Personal 3D Printing without Visual Dependency in the Generative AI Era: Opportunities and Challenges

Desktop 3D printing is structured almost entirely around visual interaction, which limits meaningful access for Blind and Low-Vision (BLV) users. This work reports first-person observations from blind and sighted researchers engaging in an AI-mediated personal fabrication workflow — text-guided 3D generation, conversational inspection, and artifact printing — built deliberately from widely available, general-purpose AI features.

Overview

Typical personal fabrication workflows span model design, inspection, slicing, and printer operation — each step heavily dependent on computer-based visualization. We examine whether VLM-supported chatbots and generative 3D modeling can reduce that dependency, and where they fall short. Following a Research through Design process, our team of blind and sighted researchers built an end-to-end AI-mediated workflow and ran two complete design-fabrication flows through it.

The blind researchers guided use scenarios, design intentions, and the selection of technological approaches from their lived experience, while the sighted researchers implemented the system. All observations reported here come from this first-person evaluation rather than from external participants.


AI-Mediated Workflow

The workflow is assembled entirely from standard, general-purpose AI capabilities, reflecting an approach that meets accessibility needs with tools that are already widely available. Hunyuan3D 2.5 generates 3D designs from semantic intentions, and the multi-modal capabilities of GPT-5.2 drive a chatbot that inspects rendered clips of those designs conversationally. All feature operations are exposed through an HTML front-end, with speech output provided by the Web Speech API.

Because no mature tool supports direct editing of 3D format files, we implemented a detour: the model's clips are semantically edited with GPT Image-1.5, and a revised 3D design is then re-generated from those edits. Alongside the main pipeline, a custom printer code generator produces three canonical orthographic projections of a given model, printed flat as tactile silhouette patterns for pre-print inspection — the rationale being that silhouette contours capture key geometric features and overall proportions at a fraction of the time and material of a full print. Models were sliced in the Bambu Lab slicer and printed on standard Bambu Lab A1 machines.


Design Explorations

Two flows were initiated with purely semantic design intentions. The first asked for a mascot character for a 3D shooting game designed for blind users — an open, non-parametric brief. The second specified a keychain in quantitative terms: a 4 cm circular disk, a 0.9 cm hole for the key ring, a 0.6 cm embossed Maine Coon cat element recognizable through touch, a 0.4 cm edge margin, and a 0.2 cm curved edge.

After generation, we inspected each design conversationally for roughly five minutes to form a pre-fabrication understanding; neither flow chose to edit at that stage. Following physical inspection of the prints, the mascot needed only a slicer adjustment, while the keychain went through the editing detour with a reference-based intention — remove the outer rim and redesign the edge after the curved edge of a waterfall display screen — effectively a request for a fillet.


Generative Modeling Limitations

The workflow made it easy to initiate personal designs, and both generated models aligned with our initial intentions during conversational inspection. Quantitative dimensions, however, were overlooked or difficult to control. The mascot's size was never specified, and only at physical inspection did we discover the print was 12 mm tall. The keychain was constrained by multiple explicit dimensions, yet printed at a 28 mm disk diameter instead of the intended 40 mm. For BLV users, such discrepancies would likely go unnoticed until the model is physically printed, since neither text-guided generation nor chatbot descriptions reliably capture quantitative dimensions.

Reference-based editing proved to be the primary constraint on iterative design. The revised keychain matched our description visually, but the 3D model regenerated from that image still exhibited sharp edges — geometric filleting is difficult to capture through image-constrained generative processes alone. Image-based editing nonetheless allows more targeted modifications than re-prompting from scratch, and remains a practical strategy within current tooling.

Reflection: design processes should surface quantitative model attributes early by integrating dimension-aware feedback into generation and inspection, and should support semantically guided, reference-based modification.


Tactile Inspection & Post-print Interpretation

Key features the chatbot had described — the mascot's cat ears, weapon, and goggles; the keychain's embossed Maine Coon face — were confirmed to be clearly perceptible to touch. The tactile silhouette patterns printed in under 10 minutes each, against roughly 40 and 28 minutes for the complete models, and conveyed coarse scale and shape effectively, but were far less informative than a fully explorable artifact. Given the low material cost of 3D printing, full prints are the better primary inspection medium for small, quick tasks; downscaled or silhouette previews remain valuable for long or resource-intensive jobs.


Greater uncertainty arose after printing. The mascot was unintentionally printed without support structures, deforming an overhang (left) — a failure that could not be identified by touch, because fabrication errors and intended design features were indistinguishable. The same ambiguity appeared during support removal: even when their presence was communicated in advance, tree supports were hard to tell apart from the model until exploratory wiggling loosened most of them, after which tactile interpretation matched the mental representation formed during conversational inspection. Residual supports nevertheless remained in recessed regions inaccessible to fingers (right).

Reflection: workflows should maintain a consistent reference understanding across stages — for example, using AI vision features to compare physical artifacts against their digital models, encoding support structures with distinguishable tactile patterns, and avoiding support generation in regions that cannot be reached by touch.
